\problem{Problem 1: Properties of the DP definition}{25 points}
% ===============================================================

This problem fills in three gaps that the lecture left as exercises, and then
makes the hypothesis-testing interpretation of DP quantitative.

\begin{enumerate}

\item (4 pts) \textbf{One-sided suffices.} Remark 2 in the lecture says that,
since the roles of $D$ and $D'$ are symmetric under both add/remove and
replacement adjacency, it is enough to require the one-sided bound
$\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)\le e^{\eps}\Pr(\cA(D')\in S)+\delta$. Explain precisely
why. Then give an example of a non-symmetric adjacency relation (one where
$D\simeq D'$ does not imply $D'\simeq D$) and explain what could go wrong if
one only imposed the one-sided bound for it.

\begin{answer}

\textbf{Why one side suffices.}

Under add/remove adjacency, one datasets contains one extra record:

\[
  D \simeq D' \iff D = D' \cup \{x\}
  \ \text{or}\ D = D' \setminus \{x\}
  \ \text{for some } x \in \mathcal{X}
\]

Exchanging the roles of $D$ and $D'$:

\[
\begin{aligned}
D' \simeq D
&\iff D' = D \cup \{x\} \ \text{or}\ D' = D \setminus \{x\}
  \ \text{for some } x \in \mathcal{X} \\
&\iff D = D' \setminus \{x\} \ \text{or}\ D = D' \cup \{x\}
  \ \text{for some } x \in \mathcal{X} \\
&\iff D \simeq D'
\end{aligned}
\]

Under replacement adjacency, the datasets have the same size and differ in one record:

\[
  D \simeq D' \iff D = E\cup\{a\},\ D' = E\cup\{b\}
  \text{ for some } E \text{ and records } a\neq b
\]

Similarly,

\[
\begin{aligned}
D' \simeq D
&\iff D' = E\cup\{a\},\ D = E\cup\{b\}
  \ \text{for some } E \text{ and records } a\neq b \\
&\iff D = E\cup\{b\},\ D' = E\cup\{a\}
  \ \text{for some } E \text{ and records } b\neq a \\
&\iff D \simeq D'
\end{aligned}
\]

Suppose the one-sided bound

\[
  \Pr(\cA(D)\in S)\le e^{\eps}\Pr(\cA(D')\in S)+\delta
\]

holds for all adjacent pairs $(D, D')$.
Since both adjacency relations are symmetric, $(D', D)$ is also adjacent.
Applying the same bound to $(D', D)$ gives

\[
  \Pr(\cA(D')\in S)\le e^{\eps}\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)+\delta .
\]

Therefore the bound holds in both directions, and requiring only one side
is enough.

\textbf{A non-symmetric relation.}

Consider add-only adjacency:

\[
D \simeq D' \iff D = D' \cup \{x\}
\quad \text{for some } x \in \mathcal{X}.
\]

Here $D$ is always the larger dataset. The one-sided bound

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)
\le e^\eps \Pr(\cA(D')\in S)+\delta
\]

only limits how much the probability of an output can
increase when a record is added. It does not impose
a lower bound on that probability, so the probability
may even decrease to zero.

For example, fix a record $z$, let $\eps=\ln 2$
and $\delta=0$, and define

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)=0)=
\begin{cases}
1, & z\in D,\\
\frac12, & z\notin D,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)=1)=
\begin{cases}
0, & z\in D,\\
\frac12, & z\notin D.
\end{cases}
\]

If the added record is $x=z$, then $z\in D$
and $z\notin D'$. Since $e^\eps=2$,

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)=0)
=1
=2\cdot\frac12
=e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D')=0),
\]

and

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)=1)=0
\le e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D')=1).
\]

If $x\neq z$, both datasets have the same
output distribution, so the bound also holds.

However, for $S=\{1\}$, the probability decreases
from $\frac12$ to $0$ when $z$ is added.
Although this satisfies the one-sided bound,
the reverse inequality fails:

\[
\Pr(\cA(D')=1)
=\frac12
>0
=e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D)=1).
\]

Consequently, observing output $1$ reveals
with certainty that $z$ is absent from the dataset.

This shows that a one-sided bound does not prevent
an output from becoming impossible after adding
a record. Without the reverse bound, such changes
can reveal information about whether a record
is present.

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{Post-processing with a randomized map.} The lecture
proved the post-processing theorem for a deterministic $g:\cS\mapsto\cS'$
and left the randomized case as homework. Let $\cA$ be $(\eps,\delta)$-DP and
let $g$ be a randomized map whose randomness is independent of $\cA$ and of
the data. Prove that $g\circ\cA$ is $(\eps,\delta)$-DP.

\textit{Hint.} Write $g(s)=G(s,R)$ for a deterministic $G$ and an independent
random seed $R$, condition on $R$, and apply the deterministic result.

\begin{answer}

Since $g$ is randomized, we can write
$g(s)=G(s,R)$, where $G$ is deterministic
and $R$ is a random seed independent of
$\cA$ and the data.

For any fixed $R=r$, define
$g_r(s)=G(s,r)$.
Since $g_r$ is deterministic and $\cA$
is $(\eps,\delta)$-DP, the deterministic
post-processing theorem gives, for any
adjacent datasets $D,D'$ and measurable
set $S'\subseteq\cS'$,

\[
\Pr(g_r(\cA(D))\in S')
\le
e^\eps\Pr(g_r(\cA(D'))\in S')+\delta.
\]

Taking the expectation over $R$, we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\Pr(g(\cA(D))\in S')\\
&=\mathbb{E}_R\left[
\Pr(G(\cA(D),R)\in S'\mid R)
\right]\\
&\le e^\eps\mathbb{E}_R\left[
\Pr(G(\cA(D'),R)\in S'\mid R)
\right]+\delta\\
&=e^\eps\Pr(g(\cA(D'))\in S')+\delta.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, $g\circ\cA$ is
$(\eps,\delta)$-DP.

\end{answer}

\item (7 pts) \textbf{Sequential (adaptive) composition.} The lecture proved
simple composition, where $\cA_1$ and $\cA_2$ are run independently on $D$,
and asked what happens in the sequential case where the output of $\cA_1$ is
fed into $\cA_2$. Formalize this as follows. Let $\cA_1:\cD\mapsto\cS_1$ be
$\eps_1$-DP, and let $\cA_2:\cD\times\cS_1\mapsto\cS_2$ be such that for every
fixed $y\in\cS_1$ the map $D\mapsto\cA_2(D,y)$ is $\eps_2$-DP. Define
$\cA(D)=(Y_1,Y_2)$ with $Y_1=\cA_1(D)$ and $Y_2=\cA_2(D,Y_1)$, where the
internal randomness of $\cA_1$ and $\cA_2$ is independent. Prove that $\cA$ is
$(\eps_1+\eps_2)$-DP. You may assume $\cS_1$ and $\cS_2$ are finite (or work
with densities).

\begin{answer}

Let $D,D'$ be any adjacent datasets.
For any $y\in\cS_1$ and $z\in\cS_2$,
by conditional probability,

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\Pr(\cA(D)=(y,z))\\
&=\Pr(Y_1=y)\Pr(Y_2=z\mid Y_1=y)\\
&=\Pr(\cA_1(D)=y)
  \Pr(\cA_2(D,y)=z).
\end{aligned}
\]

The last equality holds because the internal
randomness of $\cA_1$ and $\cA_2$ is independent.

Since $\cA_1$ is $\eps_1$-DP,

\[
\Pr(\cA_1(D)=y)
\le e^{\eps_1}\Pr(\cA_1(D')=y).
\]

For every fixed $y$, $\cA_2(\cdot,y)$
is $\eps_2$-DP, so

\[
\Pr(\cA_2(D,y)=z)
\le e^{\eps_2}\Pr(\cA_2(D',y)=z).
\]

Combining these inequalities,

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\Pr(\cA(D)=(y,z))\\
&\le e^{\eps_1+\eps_2}
  \Pr(\cA_1(D')=y)
  \Pr(\cA_2(D',y)=z)\\
&=e^{\eps_1+\eps_2}
  \Pr(\cA(D')=(y,z)).
\end{aligned}
\]

For any subset $S\subseteq\cS_1\times\cS_2$,
summing over all $(y,z)\in S$ gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)
&=\sum_{(y,z)\in S}
  \Pr(\cA(D)=(y,z))\\
&\le e^{\eps_1+\eps_2}
  \sum_{(y,z)\in S}
  \Pr(\cA(D')=(y,z))\\
&=e^{\eps_1+\eps_2}
  \Pr(\cA(D')\in S).
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, $\cA$ is
$(\eps_1+\eps_2)$-DP.

\end{answer}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{The privacy region.} Consider the membership inference
game of Lecture 1 with $H_0$: the mechanism was run on $D$ and $H_1$: it was
run on the adjacent $D'$. An attacker is any (possibly randomized) test $T$
that maps the output to $\{0,1\}$; write $\FPR=\Pr(T=1\mid H_0)$ and
$\TPR=\Pr(T=1\mid H_1)$. Prove that if $\cA$ is $(\eps,\delta)$-DP then every
attacker satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:privacy-region}
  \TPR\le e^{\eps}\,\FPR+\delta
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  1-\FPR\le e^{\eps}(1-\TPR)+\delta.
\end{equation}
\textit{Hint.} For a deterministic test, take $S=\{y:T(y)=1\}$ and its
complement; then handle randomized tests using part (b).

\begin{answer}

First, consider a deterministic test $T$.
Let $S=\{y:T(y)=1\}$. By definition,

\[
\FPR=\Pr(\cA(D)\in S),
\qquad
\TPR=\Pr(\cA(D')\in S).
\]

Since $\cA$ is $(\eps,\delta)$-DP
and $D,D'$ are adjacent,

\[
\Pr(\cA(D')\in S)
\le e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)+\delta.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\TPR\le e^\eps\FPR+\delta.
\]

Now consider the complement $S^c$.
We have

\[
1-\FPR=\Pr(\cA(D)\in S^c),
\qquad
1-\TPR=\Pr(\cA(D')\in S^c).
\]

Applying the DP guarantee in the other direction,

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)\in S^c)
\le e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D')\in S^c)+\delta.
\]

Thus,

\[
1-\FPR\le e^\eps(1-\TPR)+\delta.
\]

For a randomized test $T$, by the randomized
post-processing theorem from part (b),
$T\circ\cA$ is also $(\eps,\delta)$-DP.
Applying the same inequalities to the
output events $\{1\}$ and $\{0\}$
gives the two bounds.

Hence, every deterministic or randomized
attacker satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\TPR\le e^\eps\FPR+\delta,
\qquad
1-\FPR\le e^\eps(1-\TPR)+\delta.
}
\]

\end{answer}

\item (3 pts) \textbf{Reading the numbers.} A DP-trained model is released
with $(\eps,\delta)=(8,10^{-5})$, a common setting in practice. Using
\eqref{eq:privacy-region}, what is the largest $\TPR$ an attacker can have at
$\FPR=10^{-3}$? Repeat for $\eps=1$. Then explain in one or two sentences why
an attacker that always outputs 1 (so $\TPR=1$) does not contradict either
bound.

\begin{answer}

From the privacy region,

\[
\TPR \le e^\eps \FPR+\delta,
\qquad
\TPR \le 1-e^{-\eps}(1-\FPR-\delta).
\]

For $\FPR=10^{-3}$ and $\delta=10^{-5}$:

When $\eps=8$,

\[
\TPR \le
\min\left\{
1,\,
e^8(10^{-3})+10^{-5},\,
1-e^{-8}(1-10^{-3}-10^{-5})
\right\}
\approx 0.999665.
\]

When $\eps=1$,

\[
\TPR \le
\min\left\{
1,\,
e(10^{-3})+10^{-5},\,
1-e^{-1}(1-10^{-3}-10^{-5})
\right\}
\approx 0.002728.
\]

An attacker that always outputs $1$ has
$\TPR=1$ but also $\FPR=1$, rather than
$10^{-3}$. Therefore, it satisfies both
privacy bounds and does not contradict
either result.

\end{answer}

\end{enumerate}

\problem{Problem 2: Understanding the basic mechanisms}{25 points}

Each part below examines a common misunderstanding about the Laplace,
Gaussian, or randomized response mechanism.

\begin{enumerate}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{Wrong norm, wrong guarantee.} A student implements the
Laplace mechanism for $f:\cD\mapsto\R^d$ but calibrates the noise to the
$\ell_2$ sensitivity: they release $f(D)+Z$ with
$Z_i\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\Lap(0,\Delta_2 f/\eps)$. Show that this
mechanism is $\sqrt{d}\,\eps$-DP, and give a function $f$ (with its adjacency)
for which this factor $\sqrt{d}$ cannot be improved, i.e.\ the mechanism is
not $\eps'$-DP for any $\eps'<\sqrt{d}\,\eps$.

\textit{Hint.} $\|x\|_1\le\sqrt{d}\,\|x\|_2$, with equality when all
coordinates have the same magnitude.

\begin{answer}

Let $b=\Delta_2 f/\eps$. Since the noise
coordinates are independent Laplace random
variables, the output density is

\[
p_D(y)=\frac{1}{(2b)^d}
\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-f(D)\|_1}{b}\right).
\]

For any adjacent datasets $D,D'$, the
density ratio satisfies

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{p_D(y)}{p_{D'}(y)}
&=\exp\left(
\frac{\|y-f(D')\|_1-\|y-f(D)\|_1}{b}
\right)\\
&\le \exp\left(
\frac{\|f(D)-f(D')\|_1}{b}
\right)\\
&\le \exp\left(
\frac{\sqrt d\,\|f(D)-f(D')\|_2}{b}
\right)\\
&\le \exp\left(
\frac{\sqrt d\,\Delta_2 f}{\Delta_2 f/\eps}
\right)\\
&=e^{\sqrt d\,\eps}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, the mechanism is $\sqrt d\,\eps$-DP.

To show that this bound is tight, consider
datasets $D\in\{0,1\}$, with $0$ and $1$
adjacent, and define

\[
f(D)=\frac{D}{\sqrt d}(1,\ldots,1).
\]

Then

\[
\Delta_2 f=\|f(1)-f(0)\|_2=1,
\]

while

\[
\|f(1)-f(0)\|_1=\sqrt d.
\]

The Laplace scale is therefore $b=1/\eps$.

Consider the event $S=(-\infty,0]^d$.
By independence of the noise coordinates,

\[
\Pr(\cA(0)\in S)=\left(\frac12\right)^d,
\]

and

\[
\Pr(\cA(1)\in S)
=\left(\frac12 e^{-\eps/\sqrt d}\right)^d.
\]

Hence,

\[
\frac{\Pr(\cA(0)\in S)}
{\Pr(\cA(1)\in S)}
=e^{\sqrt d\,\eps}.
\]

Therefore, for any $\eps'<\sqrt d\,\eps$,

\[
\Pr(\cA(0)\in S)
>e^{\eps'}\Pr(\cA(1)\in S),
\]

which violates $\eps'$-DP.

Thus, the factor $\sqrt d$ is tight and
cannot be improved in general.

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{The Gaussian mechanism is never pure DP.} Take $d=1$,
$\Delta_2 f=\Delta>0$, and any $\sigma>0$. Show that the Gaussian mechanism
$f(D)+\cN(0,\sigma^2)$ is not $(\eps,0)$-DP for any finite $\eps$. Explain in
words why the Laplace distribution avoids this problem, and why the theorem
for the Gaussian mechanism therefore must have $\delta>0$ no matter how large
$\sigma$ is.

\begin{answer}

Since $\Delta_2 f=\Delta>0$, there exist
adjacent datasets $D,D'$ such that
$f(D)\neq f(D')$.
Without loss of generality, let
$f(D)=0$ and $f(D')=a>0$.

The Gaussian mechanism has output densities

\[
p_D(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp\left(-\frac{y^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\]

\[
p_{D'}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp\left(-\frac{(y-a)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\]

Their density ratio is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{p_D(y)}{p_{D'}(y)}
&=\exp\left(
\frac{(y-a)^2-y^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right)\\
&=\exp\left(
\frac{a^2-2ay}{2\sigma^2}
\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

As $y\to-\infty$, this ratio tends to
infinity for any finite $\sigma>0$.

Thus, for any finite $\eps$, we can choose
$t<a/2-\sigma^2\eps/a$ such that

\[
p_D(y)>e^\eps p_{D'}(y)
\qquad \text{for all } y\le t.
\]

Taking $S=(-\infty,t]$ and integrating,

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)
>e^\eps\Pr(\cA(D')\in S).
\]

This violates the definition of
$(\eps,0)$-DP. Therefore, the Gaussian
mechanism is not pure DP for any finite
$\eps$.

In contrast, the Laplace distribution
has a bounded density ratio due to the
triangle inequality:

\[
\frac{p_D(y)}{p_{D'}(y)}
\le \exp\left(
\frac{|f(D)-f(D')|}{b}
\right).
\]

Hence, Laplace noise can provide pure DP.

For Gaussian noise, the density ratio
remains unbounded regardless of how large
the finite variance is. Increasing $\sigma$
only makes the violating tail events less
likely, but never eliminates them.
Therefore, the Gaussian mechanism requires
$\delta>0$ for any finite $\eps$.

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{The strongest attacker against randomized response.}
Let $X\in\{0,1\}$ be one person's bit and $Y=M_{\mathrm{RR}}(X)$ the output of
randomized response with $p=e^{\eps}/(1+e^{\eps})$. Consider the hypotheses
$H_0$: $X=0$ and $H_1$: $X=1$.
\begin{enumerate}

\item Compute the likelihood ratio
$\Pr(Y=y\mid H_1)/\Pr(Y=y\mid H_0)$ for $y\in\{0,1\}$, and write down the
Neyman--Pearson (likelihood ratio) test.

\begin{answer}

The likelihood ratio is

\[
\Lambda(y)=
\frac{\Pr(Y=y\mid H_1)}
{\Pr(Y=y\mid H_0)}.
\]

For $y=1$,

\[
\Lambda(1)=\frac{p}{1-p}=e^\eps.
\]

For $y=0$,

\[
\Lambda(0)=\frac{1-p}{p}=e^{-\eps}.
\]

Since $e^\eps>1>e^{-\eps}$ for $\eps>0$,
the Neyman--Pearson test rejects $H_0$
when the likelihood ratio exceeds a
threshold $\tau$.

For $e^{-\eps}<\tau<e^\eps$, the test is

\[
T(Y)=
\begin{cases}
1, & Y=1,\\
0, & Y=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus, the attacker predicts $H_1$ when
$Y=1$ and $H_0$ when $Y=0$.

\end{answer}

\item Compute the $(\FPR,\TPR)$ of the test ``output 1 if and only if
$Y=1$'', and show that it satisfies the first inequality of
\eqref{eq:privacy-region} with equality (with $\delta=0$). What does this
say about the tightness of the DP bound for randomized response?

\begin{answer}

For the test that outputs $1$ if and only if
$Y=1$, the false positive rate is

\[
\FPR
=\Pr(Y=1\mid H_0)
=1-p
=\frac{1}{1+e^\eps}.
\]

The true positive rate is

\[
\TPR
=\Pr(Y=1\mid H_1)
=p
=\frac{e^\eps}{1+e^\eps}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
e^\eps\FPR
=e^\eps\frac{1}{1+e^\eps}
=\frac{e^\eps}{1+e^\eps}
=\TPR.
\]

Thus,

\[
\boxed{\TPR=e^\eps\FPR}.
\]

The first privacy-region inequality holds
with equality when $\delta=0$.
This shows that the DP bound is tight for
randomized response: an attacker can achieve
the maximum TPR allowed by the bound at
this FPR, so the bound cannot be improved
in general.

\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\item (7 pts) \textbf{Randomized response versus the Laplace mechanism.} We
want the fraction $\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ of $n$ bits
$X_i\in\{0,1\}$ with $\eps$-DP. Two options:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=4.2em, style=nextline]
  \item[(RR)] Each person applies $M_{\mathrm{RR}}$ to their own bit with
  parameter $\eps$; the analyst uses the unbiased estimator $\hat\mu$ from
  the lecture.
  \item[(Lap)] A trusted curator computes $\mu$ and releases
  $\mu+\Lap(0,\Delta_1\mu/\eps)$ under replacement adjacency.
\end{description}
Compute $\V[\hat\mu]$ exactly (not just the upper bound $\frac14$ on $p(1-p)$
used in the lecture) and $\V[\mu+Z]$ for (Lap). Show that the ratio of the two
variances is
\[
  \frac{V_{\mathrm{RR}}}{V_{\mathrm{Lap}}}
  =\frac{n\,\eps^2 e^{\eps}}{2\,(e^{\eps}-1)^2},
\]
which tends to $n/2$ as $\eps\to0$. Evaluate it for $n=1000$ and
$\eps\in\{0.1,1\}$. Then answer: the two options have the same $\eps$, so why
is (RR) so much noisier? Which of the two protects each person against a
stronger adversary, and in what sense? (One paragraph; we will return to this
in the lecture on local DP.)

\begin{answer}

For randomized response, let
$p=e^\eps/(1+e^\eps)$ and
$Y_i=M_{\mathrm{RR}}(X_i)$.
The unbiased estimator is

\[
\hat\mu=\frac{\bar Y-(1-p)}{2p-1},
\qquad
\bar Y=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i.
\]

Since $\Var(Y_i)=p(1-p)$ for either
value of $X_i$, and the responses are
independent,

\[
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{RR}}
&=\Var(\hat\mu)\\
&=\frac{p(1-p)}{n(2p-1)^2}\\
&=\frac{e^\eps}{n(e^\eps-1)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

For the Laplace mechanism, the sensitivity
of the mean under replacement adjacency is
$\Delta_1\mu=1/n$. Hence,

\[
V_{\mathrm{Lap}}
=\Var(Z)
=2\left(\frac{1}{n\eps}\right)^2
=\frac{2}{n^2\eps^2}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\frac{V_{\mathrm{RR}}}{V_{\mathrm{Lap}}}
=\frac{n\eps^2e^\eps}
{2(e^\eps-1)^2}.
\]

Since $e^\eps-1\sim\eps$ as $\eps\to0$,

\[
\lim_{\eps\to0}
\frac{V_{\mathrm{RR}}}{V_{\mathrm{Lap}}}
=\frac n2.
\]

For $n=1000$, the variance ratios are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\eps=0.1 &: \quad 499.58,\\
\eps=1 &: \quad 460.34.
\end{aligned}
\]

Although both mechanisms use the same
$\eps$, RR is much noisier because each
person randomizes their own bit, whereas
the Laplace mechanism adds noise only
to the aggregate mean, whose sensitivity
is $1/n$. However, RR provides local DP:
each person's data is protected before
it reaches the analyst, even if the analyst
is untrusted. In contrast, the Laplace
mechanism provides central DP and requires
a trusted curator with access to the raw
data. Thus, RR protects against a stronger
adversary with access to individual reports,
at the cost of higher estimation variance.

\end{answer}

\end{enumerate}

\problem{Problem 3: Releasing a private feature mean (a DPSGD preview)}{25 points}

A hospital holds records of $n=1000$ patients. Each patient $i$ is described
by a feature vector $x_i\in\R^d$ with $d=100$, and the hospital wants to
release the mean $\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ to an external research
group. (This is exactly the shape of a mini-batch gradient in DPSGD, which we
will study in Lecture 4: replace ``feature vector'' by ``per-example
gradient''.) Work under replacement adjacency, with $n$ public.

\begin{enumerate}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{Sensitivities.} Assume for now that every feature vector
satisfies $\|x_i\|_2\le1$. Compute $\Delta_2\bar{x}$. Then show
$\Delta_1\bar{x}\le\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{n}$ and exhibit a pair of vectors showing
this is tight.

\begin{answer}

Under replacement adjacency, two adjacent datasets
$D,D'$ differ in exactly one feature vector,
say $x_i$ and $x_i'$.

Thus,

\[
\bar{x}(D)-\bar{x}(D')
=\frac{x_i-x_i'}{n}.
\]

Since $\|x_i\|_2,\|x_i'\|_2\le1$,
by the triangle inequality,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\bar{x}(D)-\bar{x}(D')\|_2
&=\frac{1}{n}\|x_i-x_i'\|_2\\
&\le\frac{1}{n}
(\|x_i\|_2+\|x_i'\|_2)\\
&\le\frac{2}{n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

This bound is tight by choosing
$x_i=u$ and $x_i'=-u$ for any
$\|u\|_2=1$. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\Delta_2\bar{x}=\frac{2}{n}=0.002}.
\]

For the $\ell_1$ sensitivity, using
$\|v\|_1\le\sqrt{d}\|v\|_2$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\bar{x}(D)-\bar{x}(D')\|_1
&=\frac{1}{n}\|x_i-x_i'\|_1\\
&\le\frac{\sqrt d}{n}\|x_i-x_i'\|_2\\
&\le\frac{2\sqrt d}{n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

To show tightness, choose

\[
x_i=\frac{1}{\sqrt d}(1,\ldots,1),
\qquad
x_i'=-\frac{1}{\sqrt d}(1,\ldots,1).
\]

Both satisfy $\|x_i\|_2=\|x_i'\|_2=1$,
and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\bar{x}(D)-\bar{x}(D')\|_1
&=\frac{1}{n}\|x_i-x_i'\|_1\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\cdot
d\frac{2}{\sqrt d}\\
&=\frac{2\sqrt d}{n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence the bound is tight, and

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_1\bar{x}
=\frac{2\sqrt d}{n}
=\frac{2\sqrt{100}}{1000}
=0.02.
}
\]

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{Gaussian release.} The hospital targets
$(\eps,\delta)=(0.5,10^{-5})$. Using the Gaussian mechanism theorem from the
lecture, compute the required $\sigma$, the expected squared error
$\E\|Z\|_2^2$, and the root-mean-square error. Compare the latter to the
maximum possible size of the signal $\|\bar{x}\|_2$ and comment on whether the
release is useful. Why can you not use the same theorem to compute $\sigma$
for $\eps=2$, and what would you use instead?

\begin{answer}

From the previous part, the $\ell_2$ sensitivity
of the mean is $\Delta_2\bar{x}=2/n=0.002$.

Using the Gaussian mechanism theorem,
for $\eps=0.5$ and $\delta=10^{-5}$,
we choose

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma
&=\frac{\Delta_2\bar{x}}{\eps}
\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1.25}{\delta}\right)}\\
&=\frac{0.002}{0.5}
\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1.25}{10^{-5}}\right)}\\
&\approx 0.01938.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I_d)$,
the expected squared error is

\[
\mathbb{E}\|Z\|_2^2
=d\sigma^2
=100(0.01938)^2
\approx 0.03756.
\]

The root-mean-square error is

\[
\mathrm{RMSE}
=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|Z\|_2^2}
=\sqrt{d}\,\sigma
\approx 0.19379.
\]

By the triangle inequality,

\[
\|\bar{x}\|_2
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\|x_i\|_2
\leq 1.
\]

Thus, the RMSE is about $19.4\%$ of the
maximum possible signal magnitude.
If the actual mean is much smaller than $1$,
the noise may dominate the signal, making
the release less useful.

The Gaussian mechanism theorem used above
requires $0<\eps<1$, so it cannot be directly
applied when $\eps=2$.
Instead, we could use the analytic Gaussian
mechanism, which provides a valid noise
calibration for larger values of $\eps$.

\end{answer}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{Laplace release.} Repeat with the Laplace mechanism at
$\eps=0.5$ (pure DP). Show that the ratio of the expected squared errors,
Laplace over Gaussian, equals $d/\ln(1.25/\delta)$ in general, and evaluate it
here. In one sentence, state the trade-off the hospital is making when it
chooses Gaussian over Laplace.

\begin{answer}

The Laplace mechanism uses the $\ell_1$
sensitivity $\Delta_1\bar{x}=2\sqrt{d}/n$.
For $\eps=0.5$, the noise scale is

\[
b=\frac{\Delta_1\bar{x}}{\eps}
=\frac{0.02}{0.5}=0.04.
\]

Since each coordinate has variance $2b^2$,
the expected squared error is

\[
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{Lap}}
&=\mathbb{E}\|Z\|_2^2\\
&=2db^2\\
&=\frac{8d^2}{n^2\eps^2}\\
&=0.32.
\end{aligned}
\]

The root-mean-square error is

\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{Lap}}
=\sqrt{0.32}\approx0.5657.
\]

For the Gaussian mechanism, using
$\Delta_2\bar{x}=2/n$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{Gauss}}
&=d\sigma^2\\
&=d\frac{2(\Delta_2\bar{x})^2
\ln(1.25/\delta)}{\eps^2}\\
&=\frac{8d\ln(1.25/\delta)}
{n^2\eps^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{E_{\mathrm{Lap}}}{E_{\mathrm{Gauss}}}
&=\frac{8d^2/(n^2\eps^2)}
{8d\ln(1.25/\delta)/(n^2\eps^2)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{d}{\ln(1.25/\delta)}}.
\end{aligned}
\]

For $d=100$ and $\delta=10^{-5}$,

\[
\frac{E_{\mathrm{Lap}}}{E_{\mathrm{Gauss}}}
=\frac{100}{\ln(125000)}
\approx 8.52.
\]

By choosing Gaussian over Laplace, the
hospital accepts a small nonzero $\delta$
(approximate DP instead of pure DP) in
exchange for substantially lower noise
and better utility.

\end{answer}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{Clipping.} In reality the features are not bounded. The
hospital's engineer proposes: ``compute $C=\max_i\|x_i\|_2$ from the data,
then release the Gaussian mechanism with $\Delta_2=2C/n$.'' Explain why the
resulting release is not $(\eps,\delta)$-DP. Then describe the standard fix
(clip every $x_i$ to $\ell_2$ norm at most a fixed $C$ before averaging, that
is, replace $x_i$ by $x_i\cdot\min(1,C/\|x_i\|_2)$), argue carefully why the
fix has bounded sensitivity, and describe the price paid in utility. Is the
clipped-then-noised mean an unbiased estimate of $\bar{x}$?

\begin{answer}

The proposed mechanism is not necessarily
$(\eps,\delta)$-DP because $C$ depends on
the private dataset, so the noise scale
also changes with the data.

For example, consider adjacent datasets

\[
D=(0,\ldots,0),\qquad
D'=(v,0,\ldots,0),
\quad \|v\|_2>0.
\]

For $D$, we have $C(D)=0$, so the mechanism
outputs $0$ with probability $1$.
For $D'$, we have $C(D')=\|v\|_2>0$,
so the output has a continuous Gaussian
distribution.

Taking $S=\{0\}$,

\[
\Pr(\cA(D)\in S)=1,
\qquad
\Pr(\cA(D')\in S)=0.
\]

This violates the DP inequality for
any $\delta<1$.

The standard fix is to choose a fixed,
data-independent clipping threshold $C>0$
and replace each feature vector by

\[
\tilde{x}_i
=x_i\min\left(1,\frac{C}{\|x_i\|_2}\right),
\]

with $\tilde{x}_i=0$ when $x_i=0$.

Since $\|\tilde{x}_i\|_2\le C$, under
replacement adjacency,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\tilde{\bar{x}}(D)
-\tilde{\bar{x}}(D')\|_2
&=\frac{1}{n}\|\tilde{x}_i-\tilde{x}_i'\|_2\\
&\le\frac{1}{n}
(\|\tilde{x}_i\|_2+\|\tilde{x}_i'\|_2)\\
&\le\frac{2C}{n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, the clipped mean has bounded
$\ell_2$ sensitivity,

\[
\Delta_2\tilde{\bar{x}}=\frac{2C}{n},
\]

which is tight for opposite vectors
of norm $C$.

We can then apply the Gaussian mechanism
with a fixed noise scale calibrated to
this sensitivity.

The cost is clipping bias: vectors with
norm greater than $C$ are modified,
so the released mean may differ from
the original mean. A smaller $C$ reduces
the required noise but generally increases
clipping bias.

Since the Gaussian noise has zero mean,

\[
\mathbb{E}_Z[\tilde{\bar{x}}+Z]
=\tilde{\bar{x}}
\neq\bar{x}
\]

in general.

Therefore, the clipped-then-noised mean
is unbiased for the clipped mean, but
not necessarily for the original mean.

\end{answer}

\item (4 pts) \textbf{Repeated releases.} The hospital now wants to release an
updated mean every day for $T=100$ days from the same patient records (the
patients' features change from day to day, but the same $n$ people are in
every release). Using simple composition, what per-day budget $\eps_t$
achieves a total of $\eps=0.5$, and by what factor does $\sigma$ grow compared
to part (b)? What is the RMS error now? In one or two sentences, explain why
this calculation makes iterative algorithms such as DPSGD look hopeless, and
why the situation is not actually that bad (name the tools from later
lectures that improve on simple composition; you need not explain how they
work).

\begin{answer}

By simple composition, the total privacy
budget is the sum of the per-day budgets.
For $T=100$ releases,

\[
\eps_t=\frac{0.5}{100}=0.005.
\]

To maintain the overall guarantee
$(0.5,10^{-5})$-DP, we also set

\[
\delta_t=\frac{10^{-5}}{100}=10^{-7}.
\]

Using $\Delta_2\bar{x}=0.002$, the
per-day Gaussian noise scale is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_t
&=\frac{\Delta_2\bar{x}}{\eps_t}
\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta_t)}\\
&=\frac{0.002}{0.005}
\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/10^{-7})}\\
&\approx 2.2867.
\end{aligned}
\]

Compared with $\sigma\approx0.01938$
in part (b), this is about $118$ times
larger.

The RMS error of each release is

\[
\mathrm{RMSE}
=\sqrt{d}\,\sigma_t
=\sqrt{100}(2.2867)
\approx22.87.
\]

If $\delta_t$ were kept at $10^{-5}$,
the noise would instead increase by
exactly $100$ times, giving an RMS
error of about $19.38$, but the total
privacy guarantee would become
$(0.5,10^{-3})$-DP.

Simple composition makes iterative
algorithms such as DP-SGD appear impractical
because the required noise grows rapidly
with the number of iterations. However,
advanced composition, Renyi DP (RDP),
moments accountants, and privacy
amplification by subsampling provide
tighter privacy accounting.

\end{answer}

\end{enumerate}

% ===============================================================
\problem{Problem 4: Record-level versus user-level DP on a social platform}{25 points}
% ===============================================================

A social platform has $N=10^6$ users. Each user $u$ has written some number
$k_u\ge0$ of posts over the past year. The platform's analyst wants to publish
statistics about posting activity with a DP guarantee. Two adjacency relations
are on the table:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Record-level add/remove:} $D\simeq D'$ if $D'$ is obtained
  from $D$ by adding or removing one post.
  \item \textbf{User-level add/remove:} $D\simeq D'$ if $D'$ is obtained from
  $D$ by adding or removing one user together with all of their posts.
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{Total number of posts.} Let $f(D)=\sum_u k_u$ be the
total number of posts. Compute $\Delta_1 f$ under record-level adjacency. Show
that under user-level adjacency $\Delta_1 f$ is unbounded. Then define the
contribution-bounded query $f_m(D)=\sum_u\min(k_u,m)$ and compute its
user-level sensitivity. What does $f_m$ measure when many users have
$k_u>m$?

\begin{answer}

Under record-level add/remove adjacency,
two adjacent datasets differ by exactly
one post. Therefore,

\[
|f(D)-f(D')|=1,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 f=1}.
\]

Under user-level add/remove adjacency,
two adjacent datasets differ by one user
and all of their posts. Thus,

\[
|f(D)-f(D')|=k_u.
\]

Since there is no upper bound on $k_u$,
the sensitivity is unbounded:

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 f=\infty}.
\]

Now consider the contribution-bounded query

\[
f_m(D)=\sum_u\min(k_u,m).
\]

Adding or removing one user changes
the query by at most $m$, since

\[
|f_m(D)-f_m(D')|
=\min(k_u,m)\le m.
\]

This bound is tight when $k_u\ge m$.
Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 f_m=m}.
\]

When many users have $k_u>m$, the query
measures the total number of posts counted
up to $m$ per user, rather than the actual
total number of posts. It underestimates
the true total by

\[
f(D)-f_m(D)
=\sum_u\max(k_u-m,0).
\]

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{Two routes to user-level pure DP.} The analyst wants
user-level $\eps_{\user}=5$ for every user, and knows that most users have at
most $m=50$ posts.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=5.5em, style=nextline]
  \item[Route A (convert)] Release $f(D)+\Lap(0,1/\eps_0)$ with record-level
  $\eps_0$, then invoke group privacy.
  \item[Route B (design)] Release $f_{50}(D)+\Lap(0,\Delta_1 f_{50}/\eps_{\user})$
  directly under user-level adjacency.
\end{description}
For Route A, what $\eps_0$ is needed so that a user with exactly 50 posts gets
$\eps_{\user}=5$? Compute the Laplace scale parameter under both routes and
show they are equal. Then explain why Route B still gives the strictly better
guarantee: what does Route A promise to a user with 200 posts, and what does
Route B promise to the same user?

\begin{answer}

For Route A, group privacy implies that
a user contributing $k$ posts receives
a pure DP guarantee of $k\eps_0$.

For a user with exactly $50$ posts,
we require

\[
50\eps_0=5
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\eps_0=0.1.
\]

Since $\Delta_1 f=1$, the Laplace scale is

\[
b_A=\frac{\Delta_1 f}{\eps_0}
=\frac{1}{0.1}=10.
\]

For Route B, the contribution-bounded
query has user-level sensitivity

\[
\Delta_1 f_{50}=50.
\]

Thus, its Laplace scale is

\[
b_B=\frac{\Delta_1 f_{50}}{\eps_{\user}}
=\frac{50}{5}=10.
\]

Therefore, both routes use the same
Laplace scale.

However, for a user with $200$ posts,
Route A only guarantees

\[
\eps_{\user}=200\eps_0
=200(0.1)=20.
\]

In contrast, Route B limits every user's
contribution to at most $50$ posts, so
the user-level sensitivity remains $50$
regardless of the actual number of posts.

Hence, Route B still guarantees
$\eps_{\user}=5$ for a user with $200$
posts.

Therefore, Route B provides a uniform
user-level privacy guarantee, while
Route A's guarantee becomes weaker for
users with more posts. The trade-off is
that Route B releases a capped total
rather than the true total number of posts.

\end{answer}

\item (6 pts) \textbf{The two routes under approximate DP.} Now the analyst
uses the Gaussian mechanism instead, with a record-level
$(\eps_0,\delta_0)=(0.1,10^{-8})$ in Route A, and user-level
$(\eps,\delta)=(5,10^{-8})$ on $f_{50}$ in Route B.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Using the group-privacy theorem for approximate DP from the lecture,
compute the user-level $(\eps_{\user},\delta_{\user})$ that Route A gives to
a user with 50 posts.

\begin{answer}

By the group-privacy theorem for approximate
DP, a group of size $k$ has privacy parameters

\[
\eps_{\mathrm{user}}=k\eps_0,
\qquad
\delta_{\mathrm{user}}
=\delta_0\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}e^{i\eps_0}.
\]

For a user with $k=50$ posts,

\[
\eps_{\mathrm{user}}=50(0.1)=5,
\]

and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\delta_{\mathrm{user}}
&=10^{-8}\sum_{i=0}^{49}e^{0.1i}\\
&=10^{-8}\frac{e^5-1}{e^{0.1}-1}\\
&\approx 1.402\times10^{-5}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, Route A provides the guarantee

\[
\boxed{(5,\ 1.402\times10^{-5})\text{-DP}}
\]

for a user with exactly $50$ posts.

\end{answer}

\item Calibrate both routes with the noise formula
$\sigma=\frac{\Delta_2 f}{\eps}\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}$ at
$\delta=10^{-8}$ (Route A: $\Delta_2 f=1$, $\eps=\eps_0$; Route B:
$\Delta_2 f_{50}=50$, $\eps=5$). Show that the two values of $\sigma$
coincide, so that for a user with exactly 50 posts the two routes release
the same random variable. Route B certifies this mechanism as user-level
$(5,10^{-8})$-DP for that user (the classical theorem does not apply at
$\eps=5$, but you may take for granted, or verify numerically, that this
$\sigma$ satisfies the analytic Gaussian mechanism condition at
$(5,10^{-8})$). Explain why Route A's generic group-privacy conversion in
(i) certifies a much worse $\delta$ for the very same mechanism, and state
the lesson. Finally compare the $\delta_{\user}$ of (i) with the rule of
thumb $\delta\le1/N$ from the lecture.

\begin{answer}

For Route A, using $\Delta_2 f=1$,
$\eps_0=0.1$, and $\delta_0=10^{-8}$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_A
&=\frac{1}{0.1}
\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/10^{-8})}\\
&\approx61.064.
\end{aligned}
\]

For Route B, using $\Delta_2 f_{50}=50$,
$\eps=5$, and $\delta=10^{-8}$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_B
&=\frac{50}{5}
\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/10^{-8})}\\
&\approx61.064.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, $\sigma_A=\sigma_B$. For a user
with exactly $50$ posts, both mechanisms
have the same noise distribution and
the same change in the query when that
user is added or removed.

However, Route A's generic group-privacy
conversion gives
$\delta_{\mathrm{user}}\approx1.402\times10^{-5}$,
while Route B directly guarantees
$(5,10^{-8})$-DP.

This difference occurs because group privacy
is a general bound that does not exploit the
specific Gaussian noise distribution.
Directly analyzing the user-level sensitivity
can provide a much tighter guarantee.

Finally, since $N=10^6$, the rule of thumb is

\[
\delta\le\frac1N=10^{-6}.
\]

Route A's converted value is about $14$
times larger than this threshold, whereas
Route B's $\delta=10^{-8}$ satisfies it.

Thus, the lesson is that a generic group-privacy
conversion can be much looser than a direct
privacy analysis of the mechanism.

\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\item (5 pts) \textbf{A time series.} The analyst now wants to release the
vector $h\in\Z_{\ge0}^{365}$ of daily post counts, one coordinate per day of
the year, with pure $\eps$-DP.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Under record-level adjacency, compute $\Delta_1 h$ and state the
Laplace noise needed per coordinate. Compare with the naive plan ``treat the
365 daily counts as 365 separate queries and split $\eps$ by simple
composition'': by what factor is the naive noise larger, and why is the
split unnecessary?

\begin{answer}

Under record-level adjacency, adding or removing
one post changes exactly one coordinate of $h$
by $1$. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 h=1}.
\]

The vector Laplace mechanism adds independent
noise to each coordinate:

\[
Z_j\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,1/\eps).
\]

Under naive simple composition, the privacy
budget is divided among $365$ queries:

\[
\eps_j=\frac{\eps}{365}.
\]

Thus, the noise scale for each query becomes

\[
b_{\mathrm{naive}}=\frac{365}{\eps},
\]

which is $365$ times larger than the
vector Laplace mechanism.

This split is unnecessary because each post
contributes to only one daily count.
Hence, the entire vector has $\ell_1$
sensitivity $1$ and can be released with
the full privacy budget $\eps$.

\end{answer}

\item Under user-level adjacency, compute $\Delta_1 h$ for two ways of
bounding contributions: (1) cap each user at $m$ posts in total for the
year; (2) cap each user at $c$ posts per day. Which cap would you choose if
most users write at most 3 posts per day but a few write 100, and why?
Comment on what happens to the data of the heavy users under your choice.

\begin{answer}

Under user-level adjacency, adding or removing
one user changes the daily counts by that
user's contributions.

If each user is capped at $m$ posts
over the entire year, then

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 h=m}.
\]

If each user is capped at $c$ posts per day,
then their total contribution over $365$
days is at most $365c$. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\Delta_1 h=365c}.
\]

Since most users write at most $3$ posts
per day, I would choose the per-day cap
$c=3$ to preserve the daily posting patterns
of typical users.

Under this choice,

\[
\Delta_1 h=365(3)=1095.
\]

For heavy users who write $100$ posts
in one day, only $3$ posts are counted,
and the remaining $97$ are discarded
from that day's statistic.

However, this cap still gives a large
sensitivity and requires substantial
DP noise. An annual cap $m$ could provide
lower sensitivity if chosen according to
typical yearly activity, but it may
distort the daily posting patterns more.

\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\item (3 pts) \textbf{Writing the privacy statement.} The analyst's report
says only: ``All statistics are released with $\eps=1$.'' List what is
missing from this statement for a reader to know what is protected, and
explain why $\eps=1$ under record-level adjacency and $\eps=1$ under
user-level adjacency are not comparable promises, using your answer to
part (b).

\begin{answer}

The statement is incomplete because it does
not specify the adjacency relation (record-level
or user-level), the value of $\delta$ (or whether
the guarantee is pure DP), any contribution
bounds, or whether $\eps=1$ applies to each
release or to all releases combined.

Under record-level adjacency, $\eps=1$
protects the addition or removal of a single
post. A user with $k$ posts only receives
a group-privacy guarantee of $k\eps=k$.

In contrast, under user-level adjacency,
$\eps=1$ protects the addition or removal
of an entire user and all their posts,
subject to the stated contribution bounds.

Therefore, the two guarantees protect
different units of information and are
not directly comparable, even though
they have the same $\eps$.

\end{answer}

\end{enumerate}
